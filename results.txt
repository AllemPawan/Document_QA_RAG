Q: What are the main ethical concerns in AI deployment?
A: The context doesn't give a complete list, but it points to several ethical concerns in AI deployment:

- **Bias versus privacy:** Making AI fairer may call for larger, more diverse datasets, which conflicts with giving individuals control over their data and protecting their privacy and autonomy [1].
- **Harm avoidance versus risk-benefit balancing:** Requiring that harm be avoided at all costs can conflict with weighing risks against benefits [1].
- **Whose well-being counts:** Risk-benefit evaluations can yield different results depending on whose well-being is optimized, and by which actors [1].
- **Safety under competitive pressure:** Developers who move cautiously may be outpaced by less careful competitors. Safety measures may then get less emphasis than capabilities, which could lead to highly powerful AI being developed before it is understood how to make it safe [3].
- **Unclear definitions and implementation:** There is debate about what "ethical AI" means and which requirements, technical standards, and best practices are needed. Guidelines also diverge within and between sources, and it remains uncertain how ethical principles should be implemented [2][4].

Q: How can bias enter a machine learning model?
A: Based on the provided context, bias can enter a machine learning model in several ways:

1. **Through training data:** Most AI systems are data driven, so if the training data contains biases, algorithms trained on it learn those biases and reflect them in their predictions. Algorithms can even amplify and perpetuate existing biases in the data [1].

2. **Through algorithm design:** Algorithms can display biased behavior due to certain design choices, even when the data itself is not biased [1][2].

3. **Through the data-algorithm-user feedback loop:** Biased algorithmic outcomes can affect user experience, creating a feedback loop between data, algorithms, and users that can perpetuate and even amplify existing bias [2]. Figure 1 of the survey places bias types such as behavioral bias, content production bias, ranking bias, emergent bias, aggregation bias, and longitudinal data fallacy within this loop [4].

4. **Through measurement choices:** Measurement (reporting) bias arises from how features are chosen, utilized, and measured [3].

Q: Why is human oversight sometimes ineffective?
A: Human oversight can be ineffective for several reasons:

- **Reviewers lack time, training, or authority.** Oversight only works if reviewers can override the system, and nominal oversight without those conditions is weak [1].
- **Automation bias.** People tend to over-trust machine output, which can make oversight meaningless in practice [1]. Opacity of AI systems can make this worse, since it reduces transparency [3].
- **Scale and speed.** The agent may need to take too many exploratory actions for humans to check them all, or act too quickly for humans to judge them. This is the scalable oversight problem [2].
- **Human biases.** People involved in designing and deploying AI bring cognitive biases into decisions, and these can carry through the AI lifecycle [3].
